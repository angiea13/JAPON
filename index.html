01 — Country Overview

Japan at a glance

Japan is a nation shaped by discipline, tradition, and rapid modernization. Before visiting, understanding the basics will help you navigate daily life with confidence.

日本語Official Language
NaruhitoCurrent Emperor
Government & Society

Japan is a constitutional monarchy. The Emperor (currently Naruhito) holds a symbolic, ceremonial role, while elected officials govern. The Prime Minister leads the government. Japanese society values harmony, hierarchy, and collective well-being — visible in workplaces, schools, and daily interactions.

English proficiency note: While airports and major tourist areas have bilingual signage, many shops, restaurants, and local areas operate primarily in Japanese. Learning basic phrases or using a translation app is highly recommended.

Geopolitical Position

Japan is an island nation in the Pacific Ocean, located along key maritime trade routes. It borders China, both Koreas, and Russia — making it a central actor in East Asian security. Key points:

  • Close strategic alliance with the United States
  • Active in G7, ASEAN, and international diplomacy
  • Pacifist Constitution (Article 9) limits military use
  • Efficient transportation & technology recognized worldwide
02 — Historical & Religious Background

The events that shaped modern Japan

Understanding Japan's history helps explain why its people behave the way they do — from discipline and punctuality to respect for hierarchy and collective responsibility.

1868 — History
The Meiji Restoration
Emperor Meiji's reign launched Japan's rapid modernization — merging Western industry with traditional hierarchy. This era forged Japan's famous work ethic and discipline.
Religion — Shinto
Torii Gates — Fushimi Inari Shrine
Thousands of vermillion torii gates line the sacred path at Fushimi Inari, Kyoto — a living symbol of Shinto's harmony between humanity and the spiritual world.
Key Historical Events
1868
Meiji Restoration
Japan rapidly modernized, absorbing Western technology while preserving social hierarchy. Created the unique blend of tradition and progress still visible today.
1900s–1945
Imperial Expansion & WWII
Japan's imperial ambitions led to war across Asia. The atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki (1945) left profound collective trauma and shaped a strong pacifist national identity.
1945–1952
American Occupation
The U.S. restructured Japan's government, economy, and education. Democratic institutions were adopted while internal hierarchical values persisted beneath the surface.
1950s–1980s
The Economic Miracle
Japan became the world's second-largest economy through collective effort, discipline, and long-term planning — values still central to the modern workplace and culture.
Religion: Shinto & Buddhism

Most Japanese practice a seamless blend of both traditions, often celebrating Shinto rituals at birth and Buddhist rites at death. Religion permeates everyday life without dominating it.

⛩ Shinto

Japan's indigenous spirituality. Emphasizes harmony with nature, ritual purity, and ancestral spirits. Jinja (shrines) are spaces of prayer and seasonal festivals.

🪔 Buddhism

Introduced from Korea/China, Buddhism brought ideas of impermanence, compassion, and respect for elders. Otera (temples) are spaces for meditation and ancestral rites.

Cultural connection: These beliefs reinforce wa (harmony), respect, ritual behavior, and communal responsibility — the same values you will notice in daily Japanese interactions.

03 — Cultural Behavior & Communication

How Japanese people communicate and interact

From the way people greet each other to the silence on public trains, Japanese social behavior follows a set of deeply embedded norms. Here's what every Colombian traveler should know.

Traditional Attire
Kimono & Yukata
Worn at festivals, ceremonies, and temples. The kimono is Japan's most iconic traditional garment — elegant, layered, and deeply symbolic.
Sacred Spaces
Senso-ji Temple, Asakusa
Tokyo's oldest temple draws millions yearly. Behavior here must be respectful — quiet voices, no smoking, and proper dress are expected.
Modern Japan
Shibuya Crossing
The world's busiest pedestrian crossing — an icon of urban Japan. Notice how thousands of people cross simultaneously in organized silence.

Anime as cultural export

After WWII, Japan developed a unique animation style — anime — with its own visual language, narrative conventions, and emotional depth. Today it is a global cultural export present in advertising, merchandise, themed cafés, events, and tourism. What may seem "weird" to outsiders — cosplay, themed districts like Akihabara, or Shinjuku's neon nightlife — often reflects creative youth culture and entertainment districts, not everyday social norms.

04 — Nonverbal Communication

Haptics, proxemics & chronemics

Much of Japanese communication happens without words. Understanding these three nonverbal dimensions will prevent many embarrassing mistakes.

A Shinjuku ramen restaurant — even dining alone is a quiet, focused experience in Japan.
🤝
Haptics (touch)

Japan is a low-contact culture. Physical touch between strangers or acquaintances is minimal. Bowing replaces handshakes, hugs, and kisses. Touching someone on the shoulder or back — normal in Colombia — may feel intrusive to a Japanese person.

Low contact High contact
↔️
Proxemics (space)

Japan values greater interpersonal distance and embodies the concept of Ma — the idea that space between people is not emptiness but a form of respect. Colombians naturally interact at closer distances; stepping back is respectful in Japan.

Larger distance Closer proximity
⏱️
Chronemics (time)

Japan follows a monochronic time system: punctuality is essential, arriving early is expected, and lateness is disrespectful. Paradoxically, decision-making can be slow — because decisions require consensus (nemawashi). Colombia has a more flexible time perception (hora colombiana!).

06 — Edward T. Hall's Cultural Theory

High-context vs low-context communication

Edward T. Hall (1976) showed that cultures differ profoundly in how meaning is communicated — through explicit words, or through context, silence, and relationship.

🇯🇵 Japan — High-Context

Meaning is implicit, dependent on context, relationship, and nonverbal cues. What is not said is often more important than what is said. "I'll think about it" often means no. Silence signals thoughtfulness or disagreement — not confusion.

vs
Communication
Context

🇨🇴 Colombia — Mixed/Expressive

Although also relational, Colombians tend to be more verbally explicit. Feelings and opinions are expressed directly. Silences feel awkward. Meaning is carried primarily through words, tone, and facial expressions.

Hall's Proxemics

Hall identified four interpersonal distance zones. Japan generally maintains social and formal distances in public, especially with strangers. Colombians are comfortable with closer personal space — which can feel intrusive to Japanese people.

Hall's Chronemics

Hall distinguished monochronic (linear, sequential time) from polychronic (parallel, fluid time) cultures. Japan is monochronic — one thing at a time, strict scheduling. Colombia is polychronic — relationships and flexibility can shift the schedule.

07 — Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions

Measuring cultural values

Geert Hofstede's (1980) framework measures six cultural dimensions. Comparing Japan and Colombia reveals key differences in workplace behavior, communication, and social expectations.

Japan Colombia
What this means in practice

Workplace: Japanese colleagues expect formality, clear hierarchy, and consensus decision-making. Avoid going around authority.

Communication: Japan's high uncertainty avoidance means rules, procedures, and structure are highly valued. Breaking unspoken rules causes discomfort.

Key contrasts

Masculinity: Japan scores very high (competition, achievement, work ethic). Colombia is more balanced between ambition and relationships.

Long-term orientation: Japan plans decades ahead. Colombia tends to have a shorter-term, present-focused orientation where relationships matter more than long-term planning.

08 — Practical Advice

Do's and don'ts for Colombian visitors

Simple, actionable advice to avoid the most common cultural mistakes.

✓ Do

🕐
Arrive on time — or even 5 minutes early. Lateness is disrespectful and unprofessional.
🙇
Bow politely when greeting. A slight nod is fine for casual meetings; deeper bows for formal respect.
🤫
Speak quietly on public transportation. Silence is the social norm and a sign of respect.
👟
Remove your shoes when entering a home or traditional inn (ryokan).
🚶
Follow queue lines — they are marked on train platforms. Wait your turn patiently.
🍱
Say "itadakimasu" before eating — a sign of gratitude for the food and those who prepared it.

✗ Don't

📱
Don't make phone calls on trains or buses. It's considered rude and antisocial.
🤗
Don't hug or kiss Japanese acquaintances in greeting — it will cause genuine discomfort.
🔊
Don't be loud in public spaces, restaurants, or temples. Keep your voice low and controlled.
🍢
Don't walk and eat in most areas — eating is done sitting, not while strolling.
💴
Don't tip — tipping is not customary and can even be considered offensive in Japan.
🚫
Don't point directly at people or objects — use an open hand instead.
